\title{\textbf{When Does Learning Beat Reflexes?\\
A Pre-Registered Study of Reinforcement Learning vs.\ Classical Control at the Friction Limit}}

\author{Edward \\ \textit{AutoDrift Project} \\ \texttt{edwardoptimization@gmail.com}}
\date{Preprint --- \today}

\begin{abstract}
Reflex and classical controllers are excellent in benign regimes but cannot operate in
saturated, unstable-equilibrium regimes such as sustained drift, where the tyres are past peak
grip and the vehicle balances on an unstable saddle of the sideslip--yaw dynamics. We ask a
bounded question: \emph{where}, and \emph{why}, does a learned policy beat classical control?
We answer it under a pre-registered, five-arm adjudication in a high-fidelity multibody simulator.
A single deployable policy (a 72-dimensional observation mapped to a 3-dimensional action by an
asymmetric actor--critic, behaviour-cloning warm-start followed by PPO, with gated dual output
heads) \emph{reliably surpasses both a reflex floor (success $0.00$) and a hand-tuned feedback
oracle ($0.35$) on drift} ($0.856$; seed-clustered $95\%$ CI $[0.721,\,0.991]$ on $16$ seeds, whose
lower bound exceeds the $0.35$ oracle), while it \emph{regresses on the easy avoidance regime}
($0.700$ vs.\ a trivial classical floor of $1.00$; CI of the difference $[-0.511,\,-0.089]$). We
characterise this regime tradeoff rather than hide it. Two findings support the result. First, in
our own training pipeline a string of ``RL cannot drift'' negatives traced to two identifiable
bugs (an under-trained warm-start and a selection-timing error), not to a fundamental limit.
Second, the avoidance regression is cross-regime gradient interference on shared policy weights; we
rule out smoothing, capacity, and gradient surgery, and partly resolve it with gated dual heads
that give each regime its own output weights. We then ask \emph{why} learning wins and show, by
direct reachability measurement and a control-input ablation, that the differentiator is not the
drift \emph{manoeuvre} but \emph{closed-loop control at the friction limit}: a deliberate drift gives
no obstacle-avoidance advantage while grip is intact (five measurements, including the multibody
backend, survive an adversarial four-referee review), and a fixed steering rule fails even when given
the true friction, because it slides out. This yields a two-regime law---before the tyres lose grip
the correct action is closed-loop limit-steering and a drift is counterproductive; after they lose
grip, active counter-steer recovers the car whereas the classical brake-based reflex is
catastrophic---unifying recovery and avoidance through a single mechanism, closed-loop steering and
not drift. The contribution is a
rigorous, honestly bounded answer: at the friction limit learning wins decisively because it acquires
closed-loop steering control of a saturated regime that feedforward and brake-based reflexes cannot
represent; in the benign regime it should not replace classical control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A self-driving controller spends almost all of its time in a benign regime---moderate speeds,
tyres well within their linear range---where decades of classical and reflex control deliver near
perfect behaviour. The hard cases live elsewhere: at the limit of friction, where a manoeuvre
demands operating tyres past peak grip and stabilising the vehicle on an unstable equilibrium.
Sustained drift is the canonical example. The sideslip--yaw dynamics of a vehicle with nonlinear
tyres have unstable saddle equilibria at high sideslip; holding one requires coordinated steering
and throttle and tolerates no sustained error, because small action mistakes compound and spin the
car out within a fraction of a second~\citep{velenis2010analysis}. Reflex controllers
tuned for the benign regime do not operate here at all.

This invites a sharp, falsifiable question that is more useful than the usual ``can RL drive?'':
\emph{where, and why, does a learned policy beat classical control, and where does it not?} The
question matters for active safety: an emergency manoeuvre may require a controlled slide that
benign-regime control cannot produce~\citep{zhao2024autonomous}. We study it for a single policy
that must handle two regimes with one set of weights---sustained drift (hard, at the limit) and
obstacle avoidance (easy, solved trivially by classical control)---and we hold ourselves to a
pre-registered, five-arm comparison frozen before the confirmatory run.

Our answer is two-sided and, we believe, the honest one. On drift, the learned policy is decisively
better than both the best learning-free classical arm and a hand-tuned feedback oracle, with
seed-clustered confidence intervals whose lower bound clears the oracle. On avoidance, the same
policy is significantly \emph{worse} than a trivial classical controller. We do not average these
away. Instead we trace each to its cause: the drift capability was unlocked by fixing two concrete
training bugs, and the avoidance regression is gradient interference between the two regimes on the
shared policy weights, which we localise and partly resolve.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A pre-registered, five-arm empirical result in a high-fidelity multibody simulator showing
  that a single learned policy reliably beats both a reflex floor and a hand-tuned oracle in the
  saturated drift regime ($0.856$ vs.\ $0.00$ and $0.35$; $16$ seeds; the drift CI lower bound
  $0.721$ clears the oracle), while honestly regressing on the benign avoidance regime. To our
  knowledge this is the first pre-registered measurement of the \emph{benign-regime cost} of a
  learned controller---the regime-tradeoff tax.
  \item A root-cause analysis showing that the ``RL cannot drift'' negatives in our pipeline were
  two identifiable training bugs, not a representational or task limit.
  \item A lever-elimination study of the regime tradeoff that localises the conflict to shared
  output weights (via gradient surgery) and partly resolves it with gated dual output heads.
  \item A reachability and mechanism analysis (Section~\ref{sec:why}) explaining \emph{why} learning
  wins where it wins: the differentiator is closed-loop limit control, not the drift manoeuvre. We
  debunk the ``drift to avoid'' intuition with five measurements (a lateral-capacity/label audit,
  planar reachability for point, oriented, and extended obstacles, and the multibody backend;
  adversarially reviewed), show that giving a fixed rule the true friction does not rescue it, and
  establish a two-regime law: active closed-loop steering is the right mechanism both before
  slip---where the single policy nonetheless regresses on benign avoidance through cross-regime
  gradient interference---and after slip, where it recovers the car and the classical brake reflex is
  catastrophic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Reinforcement learning for drifting and at-the-limit racing.}
Deep RL has produced impressive drift and racing controllers. \citet{cai2020high} train a
high-speed drift policy in simulation with an error-and-derivative observation and a sideslip-,
heading-, and path-error reward, with heavy in-environment action smoothing.
\citet{wurman2022outracing} reach champion-level Gran Turismo lap times with a distributional critic
and very large-scale off-policy data; both are simulation-only. Recent work transfers drift policies
to hardware: a learned individual-wheel-drive policy with massive GPU parallelism and domain
randomisation~\citep{zhou2025learning}, and a reference-free formula-drift controller that replaces
domain randomisation with a learned, tyre-energy-inspired vehicle model~\citep{djeumou2024reference}.
Closest to our framing, \citet{zhao2024autonomous} use a backward-reachable-set certificate to
\emph{locate} where standard avoidance is infeasible and then trigger a reachability-guided RL extreme
manoeuvre. These works demonstrate \emph{that} RL can drift and race, or \emph{when} an extreme
manoeuvre is needed; our complementary contribution is a \emph{pre-registered measurement of where and
why} a learned policy beats classical control across both regimes, reporting the benign-regime cost
rather than only the at-the-limit win.

\paragraph{Drift-equilibrium theory and classical control.}
Model-based analyses establish that drift equilibria are unstable saddle points reached in a
countersteer configuration, and---important for our design---that they have low sensitivity to
friction and speed~\citep{velenis2010analysis}. \citet{goh2020toward} extend model-based drift
control to tracking a general path. This low friction sensitivity motivates a friction-blind
policy, though in the single-$\mu$ cell studied here it is a design motivation rather than a
demonstrated invariance. We use this theory as the backdrop, not as a learning baseline; a
model-predictive drift controller would be a stronger classical comparator, which we revisit in
the limitations.

\paragraph{Asymmetric and privileged learning, adaptation, and multi-task interference.}
Training a deployable proprioception-only policy with a privileged critic or teacher is standard in
legged locomotion~\citep{lee2020learning,chen2020learning,kumar2021rma}; our actor reads only the
deployable observation while the critic sees privileged channels at train time. Domain
randomisation and its automatic variants~\citep{tobin2017domain,peng2018sim,openai2019solving}
train one policy over a distribution rather than enumerating a grid. When one policy serves multiple
tasks, gradients can conflict; gradient surgery projects away the conflicting component~\citep{yu2020gradient},
and gated mixture-of-experts architectures route inputs to specialised sub-modules~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously}.
We use the former as a diagnostic that localises our regime conflict to the shared output weights,
and the latter motivates the gated dual heads that resolve it.

\section{Problem Setup}
\paragraph{Regimes and the policy interface.}
The agent controls a vehicle in two regimes. In \emph{drift}, it must enter and sustain a
controlled high-sideslip slide for at least a fixed number of steps; the episode starts already
near the drift equilibrium (sideslip $\beta_0 = 0.22$\,rad, target $\beta^\star = 0.28$\,rad
$\approx 16^\circ$, on a $\mu = 0.48$ surface), so the task is stabilise-and-sustain rather than
spin-up. This is one drift operating cell, deliberately bounded: a meaningful saturated-tyre regime
but milder than open-loop ``drift'' demonstrations at larger sideslip. In \emph{avoidance}, it
must avoid an obstacle revealed at a range
drawn from a frozen grid across friction levels. A single policy maps a $72$-dimensional
deployable observation (vehicle kinematics, boundary and obstacle features---\emph{no} friction or
regime label) to a $3$-dimensional action (steering, throttle, brake). The deployable policy is the
actor only.

\paragraph{Simulator.}
We use Project Chrono~\citep{projectchrono}, a high-fidelity multibody engine, with a TMeasy-tyre
sedan stepping its internal dynamics at \mbox{$1$\,kHz} behind a $50$\,Hz control interface. The
nonlinear tyre and load-transfer model is what makes the saturated drift regime meaningful; it also
makes the simulator CPU-bound, a constraint we return to in the discussion.

\paragraph{Five-arm adjudication and pre-registration.}
We compare the learned \emph{student} against four learning-free or oracle arms: a fixed reflex
controller, an entry-speed-commitment floor, an online friction-seeker floor, and a per-regime
\emph{oracle} (a hand-tuned feedback law, the best non-learning feedback controller we built for
each regime). The reported \emph{floor} is the maximum over the three learning-free arms---not a
strawman. The protocol---success criteria, the five arms, a reward-alignment gate, an
oracle-ceiling stop rule, the validation seeds, and the confidence-interval method---was frozen
before the confirmatory run. We report per-regime success on a held-out validation set ($20$
episodes per seed for drift, $30$ for avoidance), aggregated over $16$ training seeds, with $95\%$
confidence intervals computed by clustering on the training seed. The pre-registered \emph{primary}
interval is a cross-seed paired-$t$ interval of (student $-$ floor); we report a seed-cluster
bootstrap as a robustness check. We disclose that the gated-dual-head architecture and the move from
$8$ to $16$ seeds were chosen after an $8$-seed exploratory run and the protocol then re-frozen; the
$16$-seed run reported here is confirmatory against the re-frozen protocol, and the earlier
single-head $8$-seed result is the prior baseline.

\section{Method}
\paragraph{Asymmetric actor--critic with warm-start then PPO.}
The actor is a Gaussian policy over the $72$-dimensional observation with a learnable per-action
log-standard-deviation; deployment uses the squashed mean. The critic additionally reads six
privileged channels (true friction, mass and grip surrogates, reveal distance, and a regime
one-hot) used only during training. We warm-start the actor by behaviour cloning the per-regime
teacher laws, then refine with PPO~\citep{schulman2017proximal} using a bootstrapped privileged
GAE critic and per-regime advantage normalisation. Checkpoints are selected on task success on a
disjoint evaluation set, never on the teacher-imitation loss.

\paragraph{Two bugs behind ``RL cannot drift''.}
A long line of negatives \emph{in our own training pipeline} had concluded that the drift regime was not learnable.
We trace those to two concrete training bugs. First, the behaviour-cloning warm-start ran for a
single epoch; the under-trained imitation could not reach the action precision a drift saddle
demands, so PPO began from a near-random policy and stalled. A converged warm-start removes this.
Second, the task-score checkpoint selection only evaluated the policy \emph{after} each PPO update;
with many parallel rollout workers the first update could move the warm-start policy substantially,
so the converged warm-start checkpoint was never an eligible candidate. Capturing the warm-start as
a candidate \emph{before} the first PPO update fixes this. Neither bug is about representation or
task feasibility: a converged warm-start alone already drifts.

\paragraph{Regime interference and gated dual heads.}
With drift learnable, the open problem becomes the avoidance regression. We show it is gradient
interference: the drift and avoidance policy gradients conflict on the shared actor output weights,
so improving drift corrupts avoidance-state outputs. We eliminate alternatives in turn. An
input-Jacobian penalty (smoothing the policy) is null-to-negative. Adding actor capacity is ruled
out a priori---a capacity sweep shows behaviour-cloning fit saturates at a small hidden width and
deeper networks hurt. Gradient surgery~\citep{yu2020gradient} rebalances the tradeoff frontier
(more avoidance, less drift) but cannot expand it, which localises the conflict to the shared output
weights. We then give each regime its own output head: a shared trunk feeds two action-mean heads
blended by a learned soft gate (a sigmoid that infers the regime from the observation, since the
regime label is privileged and unavailable to the deployable policy). Separate output weights cannot
corrupt one another, and the policy remains a single deployable observation-only actor.

\section{Experiments}

shown as the maximum over the three learning-free arms. On drift the student reaches $0.856$ success
with a pre-registered paired-$t$ $95\%$ CI of $[0.721,\,0.991]$; the reflex floor is $0.00$ and the

\paragraph{The negatives were bugs (ablation).}
A converged behaviour-cloning warm-start, alone, drifts where the one-epoch warm-start cannot; and
capturing that warm-start as a selection candidate before the first PPO update is what lets the
final policy keep it when PPO degrades a given seed. Both are single-line changes with large,
reproducible effects, and neither involves more capacity, more data, or a different observation.

\paragraph{Lever elimination for the regime tradeoff.}
Figure~\ref{fig:levers} contrasts the candidate fixes against the single-head baseline (drift
$0.769$, avoidance $0.775$). An input-Jacobian penalty is null-to-negative and widens the avoidance
CI. Gradient surgery moves \emph{along} the frontier (avoidance up to $0.863$, drift down to
$0.556$) without expanding it. Gated dual heads are the only lever that expands the frontier: drift
rises to $0.925$ (at eight seeds) with avoidance held, and to $0.856$ under the harder $16$-seed
sample. The eight-seed avoidance CI touched zero; sixteen seeds reveal the regression is real, which
is the statistical-hardening result behaving as intended.

\section{Why Learning Wins Where It Wins: A Reachability and Mechanism Analysis}
\label{sec:why}
The headline result says \emph{where} learning wins (saturated drift) and \emph{where} it does not
(benign avoidance), but not \emph{why}. A natural reading---``the learned policy wins because it can
drift, and drifting is a powerful evasive manoeuvre''---turns out to be wrong. We show, by direct
reachability measurement and a control-input ablation in the same multibody simulator, that the
differentiator is not the drift \emph{manoeuvre} but \emph{closed-loop control at the friction
limit}, and that the friction limit splits cleanly into two phases.

\paragraph{Drift is not an avoidance advantage while grip is intact.}
A widespread intuition holds that some obstacles can only be cleared by deliberately drifting. We
tested it five ways and it does not survive. The scenario-feasibility model that labels obstacles
``drift-required'' assumes conventional steering can use only $0.42\,\mu g$ of lateral grip while a
drift can use $0.85\,\mu g$; measuring the vehicle's actual peak lateral tyre force shows
conventional steering reaches $0.78$--$0.95\,\mu g$ and drift $0.95$--$0.99\,\mu g$---both
friction-circle bounded, with drift offering no lateral-force advantage. Re-labelling with the
measured capacity collapses the ``drift-required'' fraction from $20.5\%$ to $\sim\!2\%$ (range
$0.5$--$2.7\%$ depending on the sideslip threshold). Direct
reachability confirms it: sweeping control profiles for the maximum lateral displacement reachable
before a time-to-collision horizon, a drifting trajectory almost never displaces the centre of mass
farther than the best non-drift trajectory in the emergency window---across the grid a single
high-grip cell ($\mu=0.90$) shows a marginal $+5\%$ drift edge, and even there it clears no obstacle
that conventional steering cannot (the oriented-box test below is $0/24$ for drift-only clears)---and
drift is worse on average, because high sideslip is past the tyre's lateral-force peak. Upgrading the collision model from a point to an
oriented bounding box does not help drift---body rotation makes it \emph{worse} (the rotated car's
tail sweeps a larger arc), including for angled and extended obstacles, where the geometric argument
for drift is strongest. On the faithful multibody backend a deliberately provoked drift achieves
$7$--$45\%$ \emph{less} lateral displacement than conventional limit-steering, because load transfer
unloads a tyre during the slide. These four planar measurements and the Chrono check survived an
adversarial four-referee review, which corrected our reporting (a sideslip-threshold artefact, and
peak vs.\ average grip in the re-label) without overturning the conclusion. The learned policy's win
on the drift \emph{task} is therefore not evidence that drifting helps avoidance; the drift task
rewards a different skill---stabilising a saturated, unstable equilibrium---which is precisely what a
benign-regime reflex cannot do. The drift task and the avoidance task therefore demand different
skills; we now isolate what actually drives the policy's avoidance behaviour.

\paragraph{The differentiator is closed-loop limit control, not friction knowledge.}
If avoidance at the limit does not need a drift manoeuvre, why does the learned policy avoid where
learning-free controllers fail? We isolate the mechanism with a controlled probe on an
obstacle-avoidance set (a representative friction-and-geometry sweep, not the frozen pre-registered
grid) whose feasibility labels have been corrected to the measured grip. A fixed
emergency-steering rule using only realistic perception reaches $0.43$ success; \emph{giving the same
rule the true friction coefficient does not help it at all} (also $0.43$)---it slides out (mean
high-sideslip fraction $0.51$) regardless of knowing $\mu$. The learned policy reaches $0.965$ on the
same set with a moderate sideslip ($0.15$) and a $0.035$ collision rate. So the gap is neither a
manoeuvre nor friction knowledge: it is \emph{closed-loop modulation of the action on the vehicle's
response}, which holds the car at the limit without spinning out and which a fixed feedforward rule
cannot reproduce even with perfect $\mu$.

\paragraph{After the car is already sliding, active steering is the rescue and braking is catastrophic.}
The complement of ``drift does not help you avoid'' is ``managing an already-developed slide does
matter.'' We initialise the multibody vehicle in a developing spin (high sideslip and yaw) and
compare a brake-only controller (a uniform single-brake stability action: scrub speed, no
counter-steer, and---unlike production ESC---no asymmetric per-wheel braking) against active
counter-steer. Two faithful-physics facts emerge. The real vehicle
self-stabilises from sideslips up to $\sim\!63^\circ$ at moderate yaw, so the regime that needs
intervention is narrow---high yaw rate at intermediate friction. \emph{Inside} that regime, the
brake-only controller fails in every case ($9/9$): it either spins the car out or stops it sideways,
because braking shifts load off the rear and spends friction-circle budget longitudinally. Active
counter-steer recovers in every case ($9/9$). This is the same closed-loop-at-the-limit skill as the
drift task, now framed as recovery, and it is exactly where a brake-based reflex is not merely
insufficient but actively harmful. This recovery finding is a targeted $\mu=0.5$ boundary sweep,
narrower than the across-friction, multi-geometry reachability evidence for the before-slip phase;
we flag the scope asymmetry in the limitations.

brake-only stability controller (left) and active counter-steer (right). Brake-only ESC fails in
every cell---spinning the car out or stopping it sideways---whereas active counter-steer recovers in
every cell. The same closed-loop-at-the-limit skill wins in both phases.}
\label{fig:tworegime}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{A two-regime law.}
Together these give a sharper answer than ``drift hard, avoidance easy''
(Figure~\ref{fig:tworegime}). The friction limit has two
phases. \emph{Before} the tyres lose grip, the correct emergency action is closed-loop limit-steering;
a deliberate drift is unnecessary and counterproductive for avoidance, and the classical failure mode
is sliding out, not a missing manoeuvre. \emph{After} the tyres have lost grip, active-steering slide
management is the rescue, and the classical brake-based reflex is catastrophic. Learning wins in both
phases for the same reason it wins on the drift task: it acquires closed-loop active-steering control
of a saturated regime that feedforward and brake-based reflexes cannot represent. ``Drift'' names a
vehicle \emph{state}, not the skill; the skill is control at the limit.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The result is bounded by design, and the boundaries are the point. \emph{Where} learning wins is
the saturated drift regime, in which it beats both reflex control and a hand-tuned oracle reliably
across seeds. \emph{Where} it should not be used is the benign avoidance regime, in which a trivial
classical controller is strictly better and the learned policy regresses. A deployed system should
therefore prefer classical control in the benign regime and hand off to the learned policy only at
the limit, echoing reachability-triggered extreme-manoeuvre designs~\citep{zhao2024autonomous}---a
conclusion the pre-registered comparison supports directly.

Several limitations are explicit. The study is simulation-only; we make no real-vehicle transfer
claim, and the comparator is a feedback oracle rather than a model-predictive drift controller,
which would be a stronger classical baseline. The TMeasy tyre is a semi-empirical handling model;
its combined-slip force behaviour at the limit is the fidelity boundary of the drift regime, and the
drift cell studied here is a single operating point rather than a swept handling envelope. The
avoidance regression is significant at the nominal level on sixteen seeds (seed-clustered paired
$t$ against the $1.00$ floor, $p=0.008$) but sits at the multiplicity boundary once we account for the
post-hoc architecture sweep: it survives a four-way (per-architecture) Bonferroni correction
($p=0.034$) but not a stricter eight-way (architecture$\times$regime) one ($p=0.068$). The drift
advantage clears either correction by four orders of magnitude (one-sided $p=4\times10^{-7}$,
Bonferroni $\le 4\times10^{-6}$), so only the secondary, benign-regime claim is multiplicity-sensitive. Gated heads improve \emph{drift}
reliability but do not resolve the avoidance regression, confirming the tradeoff is not merely a
training artefact. The per-seed analysis suggests the residual is
partly fundamental and partly a training-variance effect: only half the seeds reach a
both-regimes-good checkpoint, and the CPU-bound simulator caps the rollout batch far below the
thousands of parallel environments used in GPU-native drift work~\citep{zhou2025learning,djeumou2024reference},
which is the suspected reason more seeds do not reach the both-good frontier. Reducing that variance
(more parallel data, or a learned GPU-batched surrogate of the multibody simulator) is the clearest
route to making both regimes reliable, and is the natural next step.

The reachability and mechanism analysis of Section~\ref{sec:why} carries its own bounds. The
reachability sweeps and the recovery comparison search over parameterised control profiles, not an
optimal controller, so each ``best non-drift'' or ``recoverable'' figure is a lower bound; a stronger
controller could shift a boundary, though it would have to overturn a consistent margin across point,
oriented, extended, and multibody tests. The recovery baseline applies a single longitudinal brake
command, so unlike production ESC it has no asymmetric per-wheel braking and hence no brake-induced
corrective yaw authority; ``catastrophic'' is the failure of this uniform-brake controller in the
tested band, not a claim that every brake-based stabiliser fails. A production system with per-wheel
braking, active-steering authority, or slip feedback would narrow the gap---but such a
controller is itself converging on the closed-loop steering control that the learned policy acquires,
which is the point rather than a confound. The avoidance mechanism probe and the recovery study use a
single friction-and-geometry sweep rather than the frozen confirmatory grid of the headline result,
and the multibody recovery check is a targeted boundary sweep rather than a seed-clustered estimate;
we present them as mechanistic evidence for \emph{why} the headline result holds, not as a second
pre-registered comparison.

\section{Conclusion}
At the friction limit, learning wins: a single deployable policy reliably beats both reflex control
and a hand-tuned oracle on sustained drift, under a pre-registered, five-arm comparison. In the
benign regime it does not, and we report that honestly. The drift capability was unlocked by fixing
two concrete training bugs, and the regime tradeoff was localised to shared output weights and
partly resolved with gated dual heads. A reachability and mechanism analysis then sharpens
\emph{why}: the empirical superiority on the saturated drift task comes from closed-loop control, not
from the drift manoeuvre itself---a deliberate drift gives no avoidance advantage while grip is
intact, and a fixed rule fails even when handed the true friction---it is closed-loop control at the
limit. This yields a two-regime law: before slip the correct action is closed-loop
limit-steering and a drift is counterproductive; after slip, active counter-steer recovers the car
whereas the brake-based reflex is catastrophic. The useful claim is not ``RL drives,'' but a bounded,
mechanistic map of \emph{where} and \emph{why} learning beats reflexes: it acquires closed-loop
steering control of a saturated regime that feedforward and brake reflexes cannot represent---and in
the benign regime, where they can, it should not replace them.
